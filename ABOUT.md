# About Qrazy

A case study in turning a silent product on a shelf into a living conversation between a brand, a shop, and a person holding a phone.

---

## 1. Executive Summary (What is it?)

Qrazy is an admin home for brands that want QR codes to do more than sit on a pack. It is a reward-and-sales system: each product can carry a branded, scannable identity that customers use to claim rewards, and that teams use to see what is selling, where it is scanned, and whether something on the shelf looks wrong.

The core problem is simple and expensive. Consumer brands already print codes, run campaigns, and ship stock across cities. What they rarely get is a single, calm place to:

- give every SKU a trustworthy, on-brand QR life
- order those codes in digital or physical form, in the quantity the campaign actually needs
- watch scans unfold on a map instead of waiting for a quarterly report
- connect scans to rewards claimed and rewards still sitting in the program
- notice counterfeit or “fitting” activity before the channel is flooded

**Unique value proposition:** Qrazy treats the QR not as a graphic, but as the product’s field identity — a small window a brand manager can design, print, track, and protect, from first onboarding through invoices, scans, and support.

> A code on a pack should feel like the brand’s handshake, not a black square nobody owns.

---

## 2. The Problem Space & Ideation (Why does it exist?)

### The friction that sparked it

Walk a kirana, a supermarket aisle, or a distributor warehouse with a brand team and the same story appears.

Marketing wants a campaign that feels premium: logo in the center of the code, brand colors, a look that matches the pack. Operations wants ten thousand stickers by next week, some digital, some physical, some with an expiry. Sales wants to know which SKU actually moved. Loyalty wants to know if the reward was claimed or just advertised. Quality wants to know if a code in another city is a genuine product or a lookalike.

Those needs live in different inboxes. Design files in one thread. Print vendors in another. Spreadsheets of “scans this month.” A separate anti-counterfeit vendor. A finance person chasing whether the last QR order was even paid.

The human cost is not only delay. It is **loss of meaning**. A customer scans a pack hoping for a reward and meets a dead page. A regional manager hears “we did well in the north” with no sense of place. A brand that invested in authenticity cannot tell a fake from a scan that simply happened in the wrong geography.

Onboarding in Qrazy starts with names people already know — Amul, Nestlé, Tropicana — because that is the world the product was imagined for: fast-moving goods, GST-aware businesses, companies with a real address and a real contact person, not a faceless campaign microsite.

### The “aha!” moment

The breakthrough was not “put a QR generator in a dashboard.” It was this:

> If the same person who describes the product can also dress the code, order it, and later see it light up on a map — authenticity, loyalty, and sales stop being three projects.

Ideation kept returning to one picture: a brand admin who is not a designer, not a data analyst, and not a fraud investigator — and should not have to become all three. They should be able to:

1. say who the company is
2. say what the product is
3. make the code look like *them*
4. choose digital, physical, or both
5. wake up to scans, rewards, and reports instead of a blank week

That is the product strategy: **one operational story**, from “this is our business” to “this pack was scanned here, and this one looks suspicious.”

## 3. The Conceptual Journey (How does it work?)

### Guiding philosophy

Qrazy is built on three UX beliefs.

- **Identity first.** Before charts, a business is asked for its name, its story, its look, its location, and how to reach it. Skip is allowed — busy people exist — but the default path is to feel known.
- **The product is the unit of trust.** Rewards, QR orders, scan heat, and invoices all hang off catalog items people can name, price, categorize, photograph, and link.
- **Place is part of truth.** Scans and counter-fitting reports are not only counts. They are maps, recent events, and “top places,” because authenticity is geographic as much as it is visual.

The visual mindset is calm and slightly glass-like: a workspace that can sit in light or dark, greet you by time of day, and keep support and feedback within reach. The admin is not a laboratory. It is a control room that still feels human.

### From challenge to resolution

**A brand manager has a new SKU and a festival campaign.**

They arrive, sign in, and are walked through who they are: business name, optional tax identity, a short “about,” personalization (including a logo), where the company lives, and who to call. If they already know this, they skip toward the workspace.

They land on a home that speaks in outcomes, not chores: active products, QRs scanned, rewards distributed, rewards claimed, the category that is carrying the program. There is a calendar of what is happening, and a door to ask for help or to say how the platform feels.

**They make the product real.** They add an item: title, story, category, images, pricing, links. The catalog is no longer a spreadsheet; it is the list of things a customer might actually scan.

**They give that product a face.** In the QR studio they shape the code the way they would a pack panel: what it encodes, how dense it feels, how much quiet space it needs, brand colors, a logo at the heart, rounded or dotted or fluid eyes. They download a proof, or they commit to a campaign look. The code is no longer generic. It is *theirs*.

**They put it in the world.** Ordering is a procurement moment, not a design afterthought. They choose digital, physical, or both; pick a template that fits the product’s personality; set quantity and expiry; check out; keep an invoice trail and a payment status. Marketing and finance finally share a document.

**They listen.** Scans appear as a living map, a pulse of activity in the last minutes, a table of what just happened, and a ranking of places. Last scanned products sit next to invoices. The question “did anyone actually scan this?” has a geographic answer.

**They protect.** Counter-fitting is the uneasy twin of scans: the same map language, but the unit is *reports*. When a pattern does not belong — volume, place, timing — the brand is not waiting for a wholesaler rumor. They have a surface for “something is wrong here.”

**They stay in relationship.** Settings keep personal details, company story, and contacts honest. Access (when the team grows) is about who is allowed into this control room. Support and feedback are not buried: the people running the program are still people.

The resolution is not a downloaded file. It is a loop: **product → identity → order → encounter → reward → trust.**

## 4. Real-World Impact & Vision (Where is it heading?)

### What changes in a day

For a **brand manager**, Monday morning is no longer a scavenger hunt. It is a greeting, a few honest numbers, and a map. They can see whether the campaign is alive, which products carry it, and whether rewards are being claimed or only printed on the pack.

For a **sales or channel lead**, “the north is doing well” becomes a set of places and recent scans. They can walk into a distributor conversation with evidence, not folklore.

For a **loyalty or consumer team**, rewards distributed versus rewards claimed is the difference between a promise and a relationship. The QR is the handshake; the claim is whether the handshake was returned.

For a **quality or brand-protection lead**, counter-fitting reports turn a vague fear of fakes into a daily glance: where the noise is, how fast it is rising, which locations keep appearing.

For the **person in the aisle**, none of this should feel like an admin product. It should feel like a pack that recognizes them: a scan that works, a reward that is real, and a brand that still cares which city they are standing in.

### Where the concept is heading

The long vision is a **living product passport** for everyday brands — especially in markets where physical goods, informal retail, and counterfeit pressure share the same shelf.

Qrazy wants to grow from “generate and watch” into a fuller field language:

- campaigns that expire with the code, not after a confused reprint
- rewards that feel fair because the brand can see the gap between issued and claimed
- authenticity that is as intuitive as a map of home
- teams — marketing, sales, finance, quality — sharing one story of the pack in the world

> The future of a QR is not a faster download. It is a brand that can hear the shelf talk back.

That is Qrazy: not a utility for making squares, but a place where a company learns, in human time, whether its products are being found, rewarded, and believed.
